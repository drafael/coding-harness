# Pattern catalog

Use this catalog for full audits and ambiguous edits. A match is a prompt to inspect context, not an automatic violation.

## Content

### 1. Inflated importance

Watch for claims that an ordinary event is pivotal, enduring, symbolic, a testament, a turning point, or part of an evolving landscape. State what happened and why it matters in concrete terms. Keep significance language when the source supports it.

### 2. Context-free name-dropping

A list of publications, companies, or experts does not establish relevance. Keep the source that supports the claim and say what it reported. Preserve a list when the list itself matters.

### 3. Superficial `-ing` analysis

Clauses beginning with "highlighting," "ensuring," "reflecting," "showcasing," or "fostering" often append unsupported interpretation. Delete the clause or turn it into a sourced, testable statement.

### 4. Promotional language

Review words such as vibrant, breathtaking, groundbreaking, renowned, stunning, must-visit, nestled, rich, and commitment. Replace advertising copy with observable details unless promotion is the requested genre.

### 5. Vague attribution

"Experts believe," "industry reports suggest," and "some critics argue" conceal the source. Name a real source already provided, preserve justified uncertainty, ask for evidence, or remove the claim. Never invent attribution.

### 6. Formulaic challenges and outlook

"Despite these challenges, it continues to thrive" rarely tells the reader anything. Name the concrete problem, response, result, or plan.

## Language

### 7. Stock AI vocabulary

Review additionally, crucial, delve, enduring, enhance, foster, garner, highlight, interplay, intricate, landscape, pivotal, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, and vibrant. Keep any word that is the clearest accurate choice.

### 8. Fancy substitutes for `is` and `has`

"Serves as," "stands as," "boasts," "features," and "offers" often inflate a simple statement. Prefer `is` or `has` when no distinction is lost.

### 9. Forced negative parallelism

Replace "not just X, but Y" and "it is not merely X; it is Y" with the actual point. Keep the contrast when both sides are necessary and genuinely opposed.

### 10. Forced groups of three

Do not add or trim items merely to make a trio. Use the number the subject requires.

### 11. Synonym cycling

Repeatedly renaming the same thing can obscure identity. Choose the established term and repeat it when repetition is clearer.

### 12. False ranges

Use "from X to Y" only for endpoints on a meaningful progression or range. Otherwise list the topics directly.

## Style and formatting

### 13. Em-dash overuse

Repeated em dashes can create artificial punchiness. Use a period, comma, colon, parentheses, or revised sentence when that reads better. Keep an em dash when it expresses the intended interruption or emphasis.

### 14. Rhetorical colon overuse

Colons belong before explanations, examples, lists, and formally related clauses. Rewrite colons used as habitual dramatic connectors. Keep them where grammar or document structure calls for them.

### 15. Decorative boldface

Do not bold every acronym, product, or key phrase. Keep bold text used for navigation, warnings, accessibility, or an established document convention.

### 16. Inline-header lists

A list such as "**Performance:** Performance improved" repeats itself. Use prose or a useful label followed by new information. Keep scannable labeled lists when the labels carry structure.

### 17. Title-case headings

Use the document's established heading convention. Prefer sentence case only when no style guide says otherwise.

### 18. Decorative emojis

Remove emojis used as automatic heading or bullet ornaments. Preserve them when requested or meaningful to the author's voice, brand, or interface.

### 19. Quotation-mark normalization

Do not treat curly quotes as inherently artificial. Follow the document's locale and typography. Never alter quotation marks inside code or exact quoted material.

## Communication artifacts

### 20. Canned chatbot phrases

Remove "Of course," "Certainly," "I hope this helps," "let me know," and similar scaffolding when it adds no meaning. Keep courteous language appropriate to real correspondence.

### 21. Model disclaimers

Remove training-cutoff and "available information" disclaimers copied into finished prose. Replace them with a dated factual limitation only when the limitation matters and is supported.

### 22. Sycophancy

Cut automatic praise such as "Great question" or "You're absolutely right." Respond to the substance. Keep genuine acknowledgment when the conversational context calls for it.

## Filler

### 23. Wordy setup

Prefer "to" over "in order to," "because" over "due to the fact that," and "if" over "in the event that." Delete "it is important to note" when the sentence works without it.

### 24. Stacked hedging

Reduce "could potentially possibly" to the level of uncertainty the evidence supports. Do not remove legally, scientifically, or socially meaningful qualifications.

### 25. Generic conclusions

Replace "the future looks bright" and "exciting times lie ahead" with a concrete plan, result, unresolved question, or no conclusion.

## Jargon and plain speech

### 26. Empty technical metaphors

Review substrate, wedge, vector, locus, vantage, nexus, primitive, harness, surface, bedrock, scaffolding, modality, paradigm, gold-plating, ratchet, evacuate, endgame, north star, and flywheel when used metaphorically. Replace empty decoration with the mechanism's real name. Keep precise domain terms such as vector, language primitive, API surface, or interaction modality.

### 27. Feelings in place of mechanisms

"SQL you can read" and "the database stays close at hand" describe an impression. Prefer a behavior readers can verify, such as "`.toSQL()` returns the statement sent to the database." Preserve subjective language in reviews, essays, and other genres where experience is the subject.

### 28. Dense sentences

Split a sentence when readers must backtrack to understand its main clause. Do not enforce one idea per sentence when combining related ideas improves flow.

### 29. Unhelpful passive voice

Name the actor when responsibility or mechanism matters: "the compiler validates the query." Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, deliberately backgrounded, or required by the genre.

### 30. Unsupported adverbs and intensifiers

Replace "significantly faster" with a measurement when one is available. Otherwise use the strongest accurate verb or retain the qualifier if it carries real meaning. Technical adverbs such as cryptographically or eventually may be essential.

### 31. Needlessly formal words

Prefer use over utilize, help over facilitate, and many over numerous when meaning stays intact. Keep formal or specialized language required by the audience or domain.

## Cross-cutting tests

For each proposed change, ask:

1. Is the passage actually unclear, unsupported, repetitive, or formulaic?
2. Would the edit preserve factual content and degree of certainty?
3. Does the replacement fit this author, genre, and audience?
4. Is the flagged wording precise or required in this context?
5. Does the edit remove a problem rather than exchange it for a different mannerism?
